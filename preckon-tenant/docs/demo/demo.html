Demonstration runbook

Preckon, shown in thirty minutes

Two planes, one story: the console a platform operator runs the business from, and the workspace a contractor actually estimates in — ending with a drawing built from nothing and a priced bill that can name where every number came from.

~30 minutes2 presenters or 1Live, not slides 0 of 0 steps
Before you start 1 · Host 2 · Workspace 3 · Drawings 4 · Bill & programme Prompts If it breaks

Everything below is done live in the product. Slides are not used, because the single most persuasive thing about Preckon is that the numbers on screen can be traced back to a page of a tender or a layer of a drawing — and a screenshot cannot be interrogated.

The runbook is written so that one person can present it alone. Each step separates the three things that get muddled under pressure: what you do, what they should see, and what you say while it happens. Tick the steps as you rehearse; the ticks are saved in this browser.

Act zero

Before you start

Fifteen minutes the day before, five minutes on the day. Every item here is something that has gone wrong in a real demo.

The day before

P1

Open the host console and the tenant workspace in two tabs and sign in to each.

Why
A certificate warning in front of a client is unrecoverable — you will spend the next five minutes explaining TLS instead of estimating.
P2

Have a project whose chain has been run all the way to Procurement, and a second, brand-new project with nothing in it.

Why
The finished one is what you show; the empty one is what you build. Trying to do both in one project means either waiting on an agent in silence, or having nothing to demonstrate depth with.
Name it
Something plainly generic — Clinic Extension — Phase 2 reads as a real job without being anyone's real job.
P3

A tender PDF, a specification, and a drawing set of at least three .dwg or .dxf sheets.

Why
Every claim you make about traceability is only as good as the document it traces to. An empty document set turns the whole demo into an assertion.
P4

Confirm the worker container has ANTHROPIC_API_KEY set, and that the app container does not.

Why
Without it every agent step fails, and the live-build half of the demo has nothing to run. The second half of that sentence is worth saying out loud to a security-minded audience — the key lives only where the work happens.

On the day

P5

One window. Zoom at 100%. Bookmarks bar hidden. Downloads folder emptied. Notifications off.

Why
The downloads one matters more than it sounds: you will export two Excel files, and the client needs to see them arrive.
P6

Open any spreadsheet and close it, so the first real open is instant.

Why
Excel's cold start is thirty seconds of dead air at the emotional peak of the demo.
P7

If they buy software, lead with Act 1. If they estimate buildings, skip straight to Act 2 and come back to the console only if asked.

Why
Estimators do not care how tenants are provisioned, and platform buyers do not care about blockwork. Ten minutes spent on the wrong plane is the most common way this demo fails.
Watch out

Do not demonstrate on a project whose name, client or drawings belong to a real customer. Everything on screen is readable by whoever is in the room, and a competitor's bill of quantities on a projector is a conversation you cannot take back.

Act one · 6 minutes

The console: running Preckon as a business

For the person buying the platform, not the person using it. Skip this act entirely for a contractor audience.

1

Open the host URL and sign in as the platform operator.

They see
An overview: how many tenants, recurring revenue by currency, what needs attention, and a live activity feed.
You say
"This is the side your customers never see. One console, every workspace, and the numbers are read from the same database the workspaces write to — nothing here is a report that has to be refreshed."
2

Show the list, then open a tenant's drawer.

They see
Every customer organisation with edition, status, region and seats; the drawer adds real usage against its entitlements.
You say
"Usage is measured, not estimated. If a workspace is over its seats you find out here, before they do."
3

New tenant → a name, a subdomain, an owner email, an edition → Create.

They see
The workspace is provisioned there and then, and you are handed the owner's credentials and a link straight into it.
You say
"That is onboarding. Not a ticket, not a migration — a customer can be working in about the time it took me to type their name."
Careful
Use an obviously disposable name. You are creating a real tenant.
4

Open Editions, show the feature matrix, then Pricing.

They see
Which modules each edition unlocks, and what each edition costs monthly and annually.
You say
"Packaging is data, not code. Moving a feature between editions is a toggle here, and every workspace picks it up immediately."
5

Show queue depth and worker heartbeats, then Audit → Verify audit chain.

They see
Live queue and worker health, model success rates and latency; then a hash-chain verification that reports intact.
You say
"Every action in every workspace is hashed into a chain. If a record were altered behind the application's back, this check is where it would show up. That is the difference between a log and evidence."
The point of act one

Say it in one line before you move on: "One console, any number of contractors, and the operator can see health, usage and money without touching a customer's data."

Act two · 8 minutes

The workspace: from a tender to a scope

Switch to the tenant URL and sign in as the contractor's owner. From here on, everything is what an estimator does on a Tuesday.

6

Sign in and let them read it for a moment before you speak.

They see
A greeting by name, active bids, deadlines, and — the important one — "Needs your review".
You say
"Preckon's rule is one sentence: agents propose, humans dispose. Nothing an agent produces becomes a fact until somebody here accepts it. This panel is that queue."
7

Projects → the completed job. Point at the chain-progress strip.

They see
Documents → Tender → Drawings → Specs → BOQ → Estimate → Schedule → Technical Narrative → Procurement, each with its own status.
You say
"That is a bid, in order. Each stage reads what the ones before it produced. Nothing skips ahead, and nothing runs on a guess."
8

Switch to the empty project → Documents → drop in a tender PDF.

They see
The file is ingested, typed, and its page text extracted — not just stored.
You say
"It is being read, not filed. In a moment the agents will quote it back at us with a page number."
9

Open the Tender stage and run it. Do not fill the silence with apology — use it.

They see
A run with a step timeline that pauses at a gate.
You say
"It takes real time because it is really reading. And notice it has stopped — that is a gate. The chain will not carry on past a decision that is mine to make."
Then
Accept the scope summary and watch the stage go green.
10

Click into any proposed record, change a value, and save the correction.

They see
The source it came from, then a new version superseding the old one, and a message saying how many downstream records were marked stale.
You say
"I have just disagreed with the machine. It kept its version, recorded mine on the audit chain, and told me exactly what now needs re-running. That is what estimating software has always been missing — not the correction, the consequences of it."
If someone asks about accuracy

This is the moment to answer it, and the answer is not "it is very accurate". It is: "Every proposal carries a confidence and a source, anything doubtful is flagged for review rather than quietly used, and you can correct any of it at any point — including after you have already accepted it."

Act three · 9 minutes

Drawings: reading them, correcting them, and building one from nothing

The part nobody expects. Give it the time it deserves — this is where a room of estimators stops being polite and starts asking real questions.

What the parser actually read

11

Pick a sheet from the dropdown and let it render.

They see
The drawing itself, with units, footprint, block counts and per-layer lengths beside it. Schedules found on the sheet are folded below it.
You say
"This is not a preview. These are the exact facts the agents were given — the layer a length was run off, the block that was counted. When somebody questions a quantity later, this is where the answer is."
Do
Zoom into a detail with the wheel, so they see it is the real drawing.
12

Expand one of the folded tables — a finishes or door schedule.

They see
The schedule as a table, named after itself, with the CAD layer it came from beside it.
You say
"That table was drawn as loose text on a layer. It has been read back into rows, so a finishes schedule can price a finishes bill."

Marking up an issued drawing

13

Press Edit & mark up on the sheet, or go to Tools → Drawing editor.

They see
A model-space canvas with the drawing's real geometry, its real layer list, and a CAD toolbar.
You say
"This is not an image viewer with a pen on top. Those are the drawing's own lines, on its own layers, at its own coordinates."
14

Set UNITS to mm and DECIMALS to 0. Press F3 for object snap and F8 for ortho. Start a line and hover a wall end, then a midpoint, then a crossing.

They see
A different green marker for each snap — a square for an endpoint, a triangle for a midpoint, a cross for an intersection — with the name beside the cursor.
You say
"Anyone who has drafted will recognise those. Endpoint, midpoint, intersection, perpendicular. F3, F8, F10 do what they have always done."
Why it lands
Nothing else you show today will earn as much credibility with a technical audience as an endpoint snap behaving correctly.
15

Dimension → click two wall faces → click to place the line. Then select a closed room outline → Hatch.

They see
A proper aligned dimension with architectural ticks and the measured value in millimetres; then a 45° hatch filling the room.
You say
"The value is measured off the geometry, not typed. And both of these go onto their own layers, so the recipient can switch our markup off."
16

Measure → click round the inside of a room → note the running total and the enclosed area at the bottom-left.

They see
Segment lengths on each leg, a running total, and an area once three points are down. Change UNITS to m and it all converts.
You say
"Take-off, on the issued drawing, in the unit you think in."
17

Press Save to project. Then go back to Drawings and open the sheet dropdown.

They see
A new drawing in the set, sitting next to the original — the markup as its own revision.
You say
"The issued drawing is untouched. It has to be — it is what a signed-off quantity was measured from. Our markup arrives as a revision beside it, and the parser reads that one too."
Say this before anyone asks it

The editor is single-player. Your markup lives in your browser until you press Save to project — there is no live cursor and no lock, so two people editing the same sheet produce two revisions rather than one merged drawing. Volunteer that; being asked and hedging is far worse than saying it plainly.

Building a drawing from nothing

This is the sequence to use when there is no drawing at all — a client describing a building over a phone call. Do it in BIM Studio on the Drawings stage of the empty project.

18

Type the prompt below into the BIM assistant and press Draw.

Prompt
Draw a 12 by 8 metre single-storey clinic with a central corridor, then add doors and windows
They see
Closed external walls appear on the plan, two corridor partitions, and doors and windows hosted in the walls — each counted in the legend.
You say
"It has not drawn a picture of a building. It has placed building elements — that wall knows it is a wall, and it knows its length."
19

Choose the Electrical specialist and ask for lighting.

Prompt
Add lighting to the corridor and both consulting rooms
They see
Only electrical elements are added. The walls are untouched.
You say
"The electrical specialist cannot move a wall or clear the model. Each discipline is scoped, which is how you let several of them work without anyone standing on anyone."
20

Pick Wall from the catalogue and place a partition yourself. Then Ctrl+Z and put it back.

You say
"Everything the assistant can do, you can do — and the reverse. They are the same commands underneath, which is why undo works across both."
21

Press Measure into BOQ, then scroll to the takeoff register underneath.

They see
One measurement per item, with quantity, unit, location and the method it was derived by — recorded as a human entry, so no review is needed.
You say
"That is the join. A model becomes quantities, quantities become a bill, and every line can still say which element it came from. Fifteen minutes ago this building did not exist."
Act four · 7 minutes

The bill, the programme, and the paperwork

Back on the finished project. This is the half the commercial people in the room have been waiting for.

22

Pick any priced line and click through to where its quantity came from.

They see
Items grouped by division with normalised units; the trace runs back to a measurement, and from there to a drawing layer or a document page.
You say
"Pick a line. Any line. This is the question every estimator gets asked in a post-tender meeting, and it is normally answered from memory."
Do
Genuinely let them choose the line.
23

Open Estimate and point at the rate source on a line.

They see
Rate × quantity = amount on every line, and each rate labelled with whether it came from the rate book, history, or a person.
You say
"A rate that came from your own rate book says so. A rate somebody typed says that too. There is no third category where a number just appears."
24

BOQ → Export Excel → open it.

They see
A submission-format workbook: a cover sheet with the letterhead and the submission details, one sheet per division, and a summary that foots to a grand total.
You say
"That is the format you submit in, not an export you then spend a day reformatting. Unpriced items are marked 'to be priced' rather than zeroed — a provisional total that says it is provisional."
Do
Scroll a division sheet so they see the headings stay and the bill moves.
25

Schedule → point at a driving quantity → Export Excel → open it.

They see
Activities whose durations follow from the quantities, a critical path, and an Excel Gantt with month and week headers, coloured phase bars and milestone diamonds.
You say
"The programme is not drawn by feel. That duration exists because of that quantity and that output rate — change the quantity and this goes stale and tells you so."
26

Technical Narrative → Generate on one section only.

They see
A written method statement section drawn from this project's own records — and the other six sections untouched.
You say
"The part of a submission that always gets written at midnight. Section by section, so you can accept one and rewrite another."
27

Open Copilot inside the project and ask one of the questions from the next section — ideally one the client suggests.

They see
An answer with its sources listed, drawn from this project's confirmed records only.
You say
"It answers from this bid's records and cites them. Ask it about another project's numbers and it cannot see them — that is not a policy, it is the query."
28

Trace → Verify audit chain. Then switch the language to Arabic and leave it there for a beat.

They see
The chain verifies intact; then the whole workspace flips to Arabic and right-to-left, with the numbers still readable.
You say
"Everything you have watched me do is on that chain. And if half your team works in Arabic, it is the same workspace — not a translated copy of it."
The closing line

"Every number you have seen today can name where it came from — a page of a tender, a layer of a drawing, or a person who typed it. That is the whole product. The rest is speed."

Appendix A

Prompts worth using

Generic enough for any audience, specific enough to produce a real answer. Read them aloud as you type — a client watching you type their own question is worth more than any answer.

Copilot, inside a project

What is waiting on my decision in this bid? Best opener — it answers from the review queue, so it is always true and always different.
Where did the concrete quantities come from? Produces citations. The point of the whole demo, asked as a question.
Which proposals have low confidence, and why? Shows the system volunteering its own weak spots.
Summarise the submission requirements and the deadline. Reads straight from the tender document — good directly after an upload.
How much of the bill is still unpriced? Pairs with the "to be priced" cells in the Excel export.
What does the specification say about blockwork? Traces to a clause and a page. Swap "blockwork" for whatever the spec covers.

BIM assistant, building from nothing

Draw a 12 by 8 metre single-storey clinic with a central corridor, then add doors and windows The reliable opener. Small enough to draw quickly, real enough to price.
Add a 3 metre wide reception at the north end Shows it modifying an existing model rather than starting again.
Add lighting to the corridor and both consulting rooms Choose the Electrical specialist first — the scoping is the point.
Add sanitary fittings to the two toilets Plumbing specialist. A second discipline lands the "they stay in their lane" claim.
Make the external walls 200mm blockwork A specification change, not a geometry one — and it moves the quantities.

Questions to invite from the room

Ask for these explicitly. A demo where the client drives is a demo they believe.

  • "Pick any line in that bill and I will show you where the number came from."
  • "Give me a building — any size, any use — and I will draw it now."
  • "Tell me a quantity you think it should have got wrong."
Do not promise

Two things are not built, and guessing in the room will cost you the deal later: live multi-user editing of a drawing (markup is per-person until saved), and automatic re-measurement when a model changes (re-measuring currently adds records rather than superseding them). If asked, say what is true and move on.

Appendix B

If it breaks

Every line here is recoverable if you say it calmly and keep moving. Rehearse them; the worst outcome is not the failure, it is the silence after it.

What happensWhat you doWhat you say
An agent run is slowLeave it running and talk about the gate model "It is reading the document rather than pattern-matching it — that is the trade."
An agent run fails outrightSwitch to the finished project and show the result it produced earlier "Let me show you the output rather than the wait."
A drawing will not renderPress Download DXF, or open it in the drawing editor, which parses independently "The sheet failed to draw; the measurements are already read — here they are."
A .dwg will not open from disk in the editorUpload it to the project first "DWG conversion runs server-side, so it goes through the project."
The Excel export looks wrongCheck whether Excel opened an older download of the same name Nothing — fix it silently and carry on.
A quantity is visibly wrongCorrect it live and show the stale cascade "Good — this is exactly the case the product is built around."
Someone asks about a feature that does not existSay so, note it, move on "Not today. Tell me what it would let you do and I will find out where it sits."
The one unrecoverable mistake

Claiming something works when it does not. This audience will test it within a week of buying, and the recovery cost is the relationship — not the feature.
